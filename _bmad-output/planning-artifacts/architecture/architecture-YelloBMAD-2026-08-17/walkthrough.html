Walking the Yello Spine

Twelve steps through the invariants that keep Yello consistent — and the four places where the design is still genuinely open.

Yello's bet is that a private notebook, a client engagement and a whole company's shared work are the same primitive: a Space. What follows from that is the part that shapes everything — the interesting user lives in several Spaces at once, with a different standing in each. So identity is global and permission is contextual.

Every question about what someone may do is answered against a specific Space, never against the person. PRD §1

Step 2 · the requirement everything follows from

One requirement decides the architecture

Beatriz is removed from a Space while she has a Task open in another tab, mid-sentence, with unsynchronised text in hand.

  • Her editor goes inert within one second of the transaction boundary, without her doing anything.
  • On the ordinary request path there is no budget at all — the change governs her very next request. No cache may outlive a request, so a delay of even one request means a cache was introduced, which is the failure the requirement exists to catch.
  • The text she typed after removal never reaches the Space — including via a delayed or retried sync.
  • Everything she'd already synchronised stays. Revocation stops future writes; it doesn't roll back past ones.
FR-34 is the acceptance criterion this product should be judged on. If everything else works and this does not, the isolation model is decorative. PRD §4.9, note to PM
Step 3 · the design it rules out

That kills the conventional real-time design

Almost every collaborative editor authorises once, when the socket opens, then relays document updates as opaque blobs. Under FR-34 that architecture cannot be repaired — the authority was spent at connect and there's nothing left to check.

Step 4 · the requirement with no acceptable failure rate

Isolation lives in the database, not the code

NFR-1 is the one requirement where a single verified failure blocks release. The code is written by dev agents, story by story — so a rule that depends on every future story remembering to add WHERE SpaceId = is not a rule.

Row-level security

Every Space-scoped table has a policy keyed on the active Space. Out-of-Space rows are invisible to the query, not merely filtered by it.

Query filters

EF Core restates each policy independently, derived from application state rather than session context — so neither layer carries NFR-1 alone.

Composite keys

An Assignee is (SpaceId, MembershipId), so "assign only within the Space" holds by construction rather than by validation.

AD-2 · enforced in the database
Step 5 · the mechanism produces the semantics

The 403/404 line falls out for free

The PRD demands a subtle distinction: a resource in a Space you don't belong to must be indistinguishable from one that doesn't exist, while a resource in a Space you do belong to but lack the Role for must report a permission failure.

Row-level security produces exactly that without the code remembering to. Out-of-Space, the row isn't there — so the handler naturally 404s. In-Space, the row is there — so the application layer decides, and returns 403.

The distinction is drawn at the Space boundary, not below it. PRD FR-15

Step 6 · the one capability the Role matrix doesn't decide

Nobody is made an Owner against their will

Ravi finishes the Northwind engagement and wants out. The work must survive, so he hands the Space to Nadia and removes himself. That's UJ-8, and until 18 August the design had a trap in it.

Ownership moved unilaterally. Chained with two other rules — an Owner's Membership can't be removed while it holds ownership, and Account deletion is refused while you own a Space — Ravi could hand Northwind to anyone, remove his own now-Admin Membership, and leave them permanently unable to delete their Yello Account. One person could unilaterally block another's erasure.

Wanting out does not by itself get him out — the deliberate price of nobody being made an Owner against their will. PRD UJ-8

So ownership moves in two halves, and a Space now holds pending state between them. Which makes the atomic-swap requirement — at no point does the Space have zero Owners or two Owners — a two-row update against the index that guarantees Owner uniqueness.

One more consequence, and it's the interesting one. Any Role may be named in an offer, so acceptance is authorised by row identity, not by Role — the only capability in Yello that the Role matrix doesn't decide.

Step 7 · what £30 a month decided

You cannot buy the WebSocket

The obvious Azure answer for real-time is a managed broker. The numbers close that door before it opens.

Web PubSub free

20 concurrent connections, 20k messages/day, one unit. NFR-8 needs 50 concurrent Sessions per Space.

Web PubSub standard

~$49/month per unit — the whole ceiling, before compute or storage exists.

Azure Functions

Cannot hold persistent WebSocket connections at all. The prescribed remedy is the broker above.

So the socket terminates in our own container — which is exactly where FR-34's per-frame check had to live anyway. The budget and the hardest requirement agree.

Step 8 · the constraint that improved the design

Nothing may poll the database

The Azure SQL free offer gives 100,000 vCore-seconds a month, permanently — about 55 hours of active compute. Anything that queries on a timer both prevents auto-pause and drains the allowance.

Which means NFR-2's revocation cannot be a poll — its live-session clause is one second from the transaction boundary, worded deliberately tight so that a poller, or a hop between replicas, fails it. The operation that removes the Membership pushes the notification, in-process, at its transaction boundary.

Leases don't expire

A TTL would mean a database read per connection per interval. Leases hold until push-invalidated; the one-second bound is met by push latency, on a single replica, by a wide margin.

The outbox piggybacks

Its recovery sweep runs at process start and rides on inbound traffic, so it never wakes an idle database.

Probes stay local

Liveness and readiness answer from process state, with no round trip.

Step 9 · what the spine refuses to decide

The merge algorithm is deliberately not decided

The spine fixes the seam and defers the algorithm. That's only safe because the persistence shape — an append-only change log plus a derived plain-text projection — serves a sequence CRDT and last-writer-wins equally, so swapping later is a registration change rather than a migration.

The port's contract isn't prose. It's a conformance suite encoding FR-31, FR-33 and NFR-4, written before any implementation exists.

"CRDT or last-writer-wins?" stops being a judgement an agent makes on story 27 and becomes something the tests decide. AD-12

Last-writer-wins cannot pass that suite: it overwrites one of two people editing different parts of the same description, and after a five-minute disconnect it can preserve your changes or theirs, never both. Adopting it would be a PRD amendment, not an architecture decision.

Step 10 · where two good choices collided

C# everywhere, and a CRDT that won't run there

Two decisions pulled against each other: use a proven CRDT rather than hand-roll one, and C# everywhere, including the browser.

YDotNet

The good binding, over Rust yrs. Ships Windows, Linux and macOS natives only — no WebAssembly target. Fine on the server, impossible in Blazor.

Ycs

The only pure-C# port, so the only one that could run in WASM. No NuGet package, no releases at all, tested against a Yjs six minor versions old.

Shared C# merge

One source compiled twice — WASM for the client, native for the server. The only option giving a single merge implementation rather than two that must stay compatible forever.

The rich-text complexity that makes CRDTs brutal doesn't apply here: the PRD only ever asks for plain text, ten editors, small documents.

Step 11 · what the gate caught

Ten findings, three of them in my own draft

The gate ran twice — once when the spine was written, once when the PRD moved underneath it. The second run had no independent reviewers available, so it ran inline; the property it relies on, a fresh reader finding what the author talked past, was absent. Its findings stand on cited sources instead.

A live data-leak class

Pooled connections don't clear session state on return, and a documented SQL Server defect lets a parallel plan reading session context return another tenant's rows under RLS — silently, successfully. Fixed with per-unit-of-work context, MAXDOP = 1, and a pooled-reuse isolation test.

UUIDv7 was wrong

SQL Server orders uniqueidentifier low-order bytes first, so a v7 fragments an index exactly like a v4. Corrected to sequential GUIDs.

The ownership swap was wrong

I claimed SQL Server evaluates a unique index at statement completion, so order wouldn't matter. It has no deferred enforcement at all — that's PostgreSQL and Oracle. A single statement could promote first, transiently write two Owners, and fail. Plan-dependent, which is worse than reliably broken.

Two claims, two defects

Both web-lens findings in this spine were confident assertions about SQL Server index behaviour, and both were wrong. Treat any claim about this engine here as unverified until checked. The failure mode isn't ignorance — it's fluency.

Three more seams closed

Expiry checkable against two different clocks with a race between check and transition; no fixed status for a state-conflict refusal, so three slices could return 404, 409 and 500 while all obeying every rule; and a read path that invited a cross-Space offers inbox, quietly breaching AD-24.

A requirement that landed nowhere

An invitation token must identify an offer, never authorise acceptance — closing acceptance by mail scanners, prefetchers and forwarded links. The spine said nothing, and a route that mutates on a bare fetch is exactly the first shape you'd reach for.

Four seams closed

A lease TTL that contradicted the no-polling rule; an outbox that needed a banned poll; an unowned projection write that could race; and two paths that could each provision a Personal Space.

Four requirements recovered

Account-existence non-disclosure; the Account-scoped surfaces that RLS had no home for; NFR-8's bounds as refusals; and the deletion behaviours where a cascade is the obvious reach and every one of them is wrong.

Step 12 · what to talk about

Four things still open

  • Cold start against NFR-5. Scale-to-zero plus auto-pause means sparse traffic makes most requests cold, against a 300 ms p95 budget. Pin a warm replica for ~$12–15/month, or declare NFR-5 measured-warm. Either is defensible; silence isn't.
  • Is Azure SQL affected by the session-context defect at all? MAXDOP = 1 is cheap insurance at this scale, but the documented trace-flag workaround isn't available on Azure SQL, and nobody has confirmed the exposure.
  • The merge algorithm. Deferred on purpose. The conformance suite decides admissibility — but someone still has to write it, and it should be written before the epic, not during.
  • Where Nadia accepts. The architecture fixes that a pending offer is read inside the Space, at whatever Role the recipient holds — and rules out a cross-Space inbox. But the PRD scopes Space settings to Owner and Admin, and Nadia is a Member when she accepts. So a Member can hold a capability with no surface to exercise it on. That's a product and UX call, not an architecture one.

The PRD's own six open questions are now closed — all of them, on 18 August. Three changed this spine: ownership became an offer, the five-second revocation budget became two clauses, and invitation acceptance became a deliberate act. The NFR-8 bounds were confirmed rather than revised.
