Yello — colour theme candidates

Five directions, chosen to differ in register rather than hue, so the decision is about what Yello should feel like rather than which blue. Light and dark shown as peers — both are in scope, so every token carries a pair. Nothing here is a recommendation; pick one, mix two, or reject all five and I’ll render another round.

Every panel shows the same nine semantic roles, each earning its place from a Yello requirement rather than from decoration:
  • page / column / card — the three Board grounds (FR-28). Yello’s Board is a view of a Project, columns are Statuses.
  • text / text-muted — body and secondary. Both must clear WCAG 2.1 AA (NFR-9).
  • accent — the one colour that means “this acts”.
  • focus — separate from accent on purpose. NFR-9 requires every Board pointer move to have a keyboard equivalent, so the focus ring is load-bearing, not a default.
  • presence — FR-32. Shown here paired with a text count, never colour alone, because NFR-9 forbids conveying it by colour or position alone.
  • role — UJ-4 requires the acting Role legible at all times. It is a permanent chrome element, not a badge that appears sometimes.
  • danger — deletion is irreversible with no trash (§6.2), and you chose to keep it that way, so this token does real work.
Each snippet shows a card focused (keyboard path), a card lifted mid-drag with a drop zone (pointer and long-press touch path), and a destructive line — the three states the palette has to survive.

A — Studio

Professional, unsurprising, immediately legible to anyone who has ever used a board tool.

The closest of the five to the reference product’s visual language: cool blue accent, white cards floating on a tinted column ground, soft one-pixel lift. Lowest learning cost and the most “of course” — which is also the argument against it, since Yello would read as a Trello clone rather than its own product.

B — Slate

Quiet, tool-like, gets out of the way. The palette that doesn’t want your attention.

Near-zero chroma: the interface is greys and ink, and the only saturated colours in the whole product are the ones carrying meaning — focus, presence, danger. The accent is near-black rather than a hue. Argument for it: SM-C2 makes time-in-application a counter-metric, and UJ-1’s success condition is Ravi closing the tab. A palette with no personality to admire is a palette you leave. Argument against: it may read as unfinished rather than restrained.

C — Paper

Calm, warm, unhurried. Low-stimulus by design.

Warm neutral grounds and ink-dark text instead of the cool greys every task tool uses, with a deep green-teal doing accent duty. The register is closer to a notebook than a dashboard. Distinguishes Yello most sharply from the reference product without going loud. Argument against: warmth reads as “personal”, which may undersell the client-engagement and multi-Space half of the product.

D — Signal

Decisive and sharp. Structure drawn with borders, not shadows.

No lift, no soft edges — cards are defined by a visible hairline against a plain ground, and the drag state uses a hard offset rather than a blur. Highest contrast of the five and the most density-tolerant, which is the direct argument for it: FR-28 requires the Board to stay usable at 5,000 Tasks in a Project, and shadow-based card separation is the first thing that collapses when rows get tight. Argument against: it is the least warm and can read as severe.

E — Dusk

Focused and screen-native. Designed dark-first, with light as the derived peer.

Deep blue-violet grounds rather than neutral greys, so dark mode is the design’s home rather than an inversion of it. The one direction here where choosing dark costs nothing, because it was drawn dark and the light theme is the adaptation. Argument against: it is the most stylistically committed of the five, and a strongly tinted ground competes with user-chosen Label colours for attention.
